# Chronology and historical interpretation

## Regional administration, diplomacy and exchange

Imperial Bureaucracy and Provincial Government are comparative gameplay labels, not identical institutions across empires. Achaemenid satrapies had regional fiscal and military obligations; Persepolis tablets document particular food transactions and travel entitlements. Their survival does not establish uniform practice across Persia. Mauryan Rajjukas and Dhamma Mahamatras appear in Ashoka's prescriptions; Roman proconsuls arose in a different magistracy and provincial-command context. No simple line of Persian-to-Roman institutional copying is asserted.

Ashoka's Pillar Edicts IV and VII describe justice, officers and royal communications. UNESCO documents the surviving Lumbini pillar, attributed to 249 BCE; this does not settle the Buddha's birth chronology or reconstruct all Mauryan provinces. The hosted English edict rendering has translation uncertainties and is not an inspected original or complete critical edition. Prescriptive inscriptions attest royal intentions and claims, not automatic local compliance.

Qin centralization (221-206 BCE) and Sima Qian's Han court work under Emperor Wu illustrate distinct administrative and scholarly contexts. His Records were completed around 94 BCE, not at the beginning of historical writing worldwide. The later Sui examination system must not be projected onto Qin/Han government. Greek-associated Rhetoric symbolizes persuasion, not a necessary origin of Asian diplomacy; Roman senatorial foreign relations retain their own setting.

Zhang Qian's Han mission sought an alliance; Ashoka's Rock Edict XIII names Hellenistic kings within a claim of moral influence, not independently proven foreign conversion. Envoys were not necessarily modern permanent embassies. Caravan routes involved local stages and intermediaries, not a single transcontinental road opened on one date. Cultural Exchange requires neither diplomacy nor peaceful or equal encounters in real history. These five optional recipes select associations, not universal causal dependencies. Claim-specific citations and passage-access limits are in the [research registry](../src/game/content/research.json).

## Scholarship, textual memory and geographic representations

Library and Rhetoric are reused, not newly invented by the expansion. Historical Writing, Cartography and Commentary are optional Age V labels tied to earlier writing, scribal and representational practices. Bronze Age Writing, Scroll, Scribe, Archive and Map and Iron Age Alphabet and Manuscript keep their gameplay chronology; Age V is not the beginning of record-making or knowledge.

Ancient libraries involved collection, copying, arrangement and consultation. Tablet colophons and excavated material support discussion of preservation intentions, while material survival and later copying select what remains. Libraries were not universally public lending institutions. The existing Library entry preserves earlier Mesopotamian precedents and uncertainty about Alexandria.

Herodotus's narrative aims are textual evidence, not a guarantee that every report is eyewitness or correct. Darius's surviving Behistun relief and trilingual inscription attest royal self-presentation; the accession narrative remains contested. These different witnesses are not interchangeable with modern critical historical writing.

A Babylonian world-map tablet combines inscriptions, named places and a schematic diagram; its cosmological purpose is an interpretation and its likely findspot is qualified. Ptolemy's mathematical geography used coordinates but uneven data, not modern survey accuracy. No later illustrated copy is identified as his original ancient drawing. The museum description was accessed through a credited republication because the original catalog was blocked.

Commentary could justify, question and extend inherited work. Liu Hui's 263 CE commentary and the earlier explanatory passages described in the Edwin Smith Papyrus are distinct examples, not a single genre or a proven transmission sequence. Neither papyrus scrolls nor Greek formal logic were universal prerequisites; the recipe is a gameplay association. Source-specific citations and access limits are recorded in the [research registry](../src/game/content/research.json).

## Commerce, money and fiscal institutions

Merchant Network and Customs Duty are optional Age V discoveries, not mandatory stages of economic development. Existing Coinage, Mint and Maritime Trade histories now compare early western Anatolian electrum (a gold-silver alloy), Indian punch-marked silver pieces and Chinese cast copper-alloy forms. Weight standards, denominations and issuing marks connect coinage to metalworking and standardized exchange; they did not guarantee universal acceptance or remove regional monetary differences. Trade preceded coins and did not universally require them.

The Merchant Network label describes relationships reconstructed from texts, agents, cargo and artifact distributions, not one ancient organization. Networks predate Roman infrastructure and include inland and sea connections. Amphora evidence illuminates Mediterranean transport without identifying every producer, intermediary or direct route.

Customs could apply at inland stations and fiscal boundaries rather than modern national borders. Merola's scholarly review of the Ephesian customs law distinguishes its final publication in 62 CE from disputed earlier layers. Healey's institutional abstract describes Palmyra's 137 CE municipal tariff as local taxes rather than caravan trade. The Atlas preserves that distinction and discloses that the underlying critical edition and complete handbook chapter were not accessed. Neither inscription dates the invention of taxation, establishes a universal rate or requires coin payment. Claim-specific citations and access receipts are in the [research registry](../src/game/content/research.json).

The **Three-Age System** originated in the classification of Scandinavian archaeological collections by Christian Jurgensen Thomsen, published in 1836. It remains useful in particular European, Near Eastern, and other archaeological traditions, but is not a universal ladder of human development. Some regions have different sequences or no distinct Bronze Age. Stone, wood, bone, copper, bronze, and iron technologies also overlap rather than replace one another instantaneously.

**Earliest evidence is not widespread adoption.** A rare artifact, experimental process, or first surviving written reference does not establish when a technology became common. Nor does absence of surviving evidence prove absence of a practice. Page placement may represent an intensified craft tradition or an optional later refinement, not invention. Dates and political labels are geographically dependent; Classical Antiquity can overlap iron-using societies rather than follow a worldwide Iron Age boundary.

The fifth selector button shows the approximate c. 800 BCE–c. 500 CE gameplay frame. Its regional-scope tooltip, the Guide's About Classical Antiquity introduction and individually dated Atlas records carry the historical qualification; its position after Iron Age and its progress counters express game organization, not a synchronized worldwide transition. Completing all five required landmarks and six discoveries does not certify historical knowledge or require every optional civic, engineering, commercial or inquiry branch.

## Engineering materials, precedents and operation

The optional engineering branch reuses existing Arch, Aqueduct and Amphitheater, rather than presenting them as newly Roman inventions. Earlier waterworks include Bronze Age conduits, Assyrian works and Greek tunnels; Persian qanats are groundwater galleries, not identical to every aqueduct. The Arch history retains pre-Roman and Etruscan precedents. Amphitheaters are distinct from Greek theaters; Roman monumental construction does not erase earlier wooden or earth-supported forms, violence or hierarchy.

Limestone calcination transforms calcium carbonate into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide under sustained high heat. Hydration of quicklime forms slaked lime, calcium hydroxide, and releases heat. Ordinary air-lime mortar hardens through carbonation; lime-pozzolan reactions instead form cementitious hydrates. These distinct reactions are not interchangeable, and not all lime mortar sets under water. The Atlas includes caustic-material cautions, not practical handling instructions.

Stone Age placement of Slaked Lime and Lime Mortar is a regional gameplay choice informed by prehistoric lime-plaster traditions. It does not establish a first pure hydroxide find, the first masonry-joint mortar, or universal adoption. Volcanic Ash is natural eruptive rock, mineral and glass debris; Rock + Heat symbolizes a geological encounter, not an actual manufacturing process or burning plant matter.

Concrete represents Roman lime-pozzolan binder with coarse aggregate, not modern Portland-cement concrete made using ground clinker. Its two-input recipe omits coarse aggregate, additional water, placement and curing. Archaeological and experimental hot-mixing research suggests alternatives involving quicklime, so the playable pre-slaked chain is not universal. Site-specific lime clasts and laboratory crack-sealing results do not imply all ancient concrete self-heals or outlasts all modern concrete.

Aqueducts generally conveyed water by gravity with surveyed downward gradients; arch supports did not pump water, and there was no single universal slope. Conduits, tunnels and pipelines, settling tanks, fountains and distribution infrastructure matter as well as spectacular arcades. Debris removal, leak repair and maintaining supply during repairs were continuing work. Fountains, latrines, baths and some wealthy households did not imply equal private supply for every resident.

Road Network evokes connected routes, drainage, repair, trade and imperial control, not a Roman invention of roads or an inevitable need for drawn maps. Public Bath distinguishes Greek precedents and varied local water supplies from Roman monumental examples. Hypocausts moved hot air beneath raised floors and through wall spaces; they were not modern radiators. Terracotta heating components are not the game's unfired Mud Brick. Neither aqueduct supply nor equal access was universal.

Passage-specific references and access limits are listed in the [engineering research audit](research.md#engineering-materials-and-infrastructure-audit). Existing age gates, landmarks, collections and challenges are gameplay contracts, not historical claims about necessary technological sequences.

## Civic membership and institutional distinctions

The optional Citizenship discovery compares political membership without imposing modern universal suffrage on antiquity. Aristotle's definition concerns deliberative or judicial participation, not everyone living in a city; the Atlas distinguishes his normative theory from a survey of all Greek practice. Greek assembly, council and jury participation is not interchangeable with Roman magistracies, the elite Senate and citizen assemblies. Legal or social membership and effective political power must also be distinguished. Exclusion by gender, enslavement, foreign status and other local eligibility rules is described, not endorsed.

Agora remains a multifunctional Greek civic space; Forum remains a distinct Roman civic, legal, religious and commercial space, not an Agora renamed by adding Law. Polity is reused for the broad political-community concept, Senate for a specifically Roman council and Rhetoric for civic persuasion. Assemblies are explained as institutions rather than collapsed into Senate or a universal Government element. The new person-and-law association is symbolic, not an automatic grant of citizenship or evidence that written law was universally required.

The passage-specific reference is Fred Miller, [Aristotle's Political Theory](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-politics/), especially section 3, alongside the existing Roman institutional sources in the Atlas. Fourth-century Greek theory and the conventional Roman republican span date regional contexts, not the first citizenship. No worldwide origin or synchronized civic development is inferred.

## Classical elements: historical theory, symbolic gameplay

The four-element theory is **documented historical natural philosophy**, not a fictional invention of the game. **Empedocles of Acragas**, in Greek Sicily, was a major early formulator of the four-root theory in the **fifth century BCE**: fire, water, earth and air were fundamental constituents of nature. The surviving poetic fragments and later accounts are textual evidence for the theory, not scientific proof of its validity. Precise biographical dates and some fragment interpretations remain uncertain.

**Aristotle** developed an account in the **fourth century BCE** linking elemental bodies to qualities: fire hot/dry, air hot/moist, water cold/moist, and earth cold/dry. His account distinguishes elementary bodies from ordinary examples; it should not be conflated with Empedocles' roots and forces or modern chemical composition. The air root discussed in Empedocles' scholarship is *aither*, upper atmospheric air, not simply modern breathing air.

Empedocles' **roots** are enduring constituents: mixing and separation under Love and Strife explain change without creating or destroying the roots. Aristotle's four qualities form the opposed pairs hot/cold and wet (translated as **moist**)/dry. Fire is hot/dry, water cold/wet, earth cold/dry and air hot/wet. These are attributed historical classifications, **not modern scientific measurements** of temperature or humidity.

Aristotelian matter theory influenced later natural philosophy, and later alchemists used the four-element framework to reason about material transformation. Subsequent chemical accounts revised these categories; historical influence is neither proof of successful transmutation into gold nor evidence that all alchemical traditions were identical. The reviewed passages in [Philosophy of Chemistry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chemistry/) (Michael Weisberg, Paul Needham and Robin Hendry; first published 14 March 2011, revised 11 March 2026) support conceptual reception and revision. Mark Cartwright's [Alchemy](https://www.worldhistory.org/Alchemy/) (24 August 2023) supplies supplementary context, not a replacement for Aristotle's text or verification of every claim in that article.

The four starter records are classified as **historical-model**, with mandatory verified citations. Their existing usage field accommodates explanatory concepts, and their evidence field accommodates philosophical texts. Atlas headings distinguish **Concept and use** and **Intellectual-history evidence** from manufacture and excavated material. Metal remains a symbolic Origins entry. Content schema version 2 and save version 7 are unchanged.

### Passages, translation and chronology limits

- Kingsley and Parry's SEP *Empedocles*, **Life and Writings** and **Roots and Forces**, discusses uncertain biographical dates and transmitted fragments **B6** (the four roots) and **B17** (mixing, separation and enduring constituents). This is modern scholarship interpreting fragmentary ancient texts; divine-name correspondences remain debated.
- Shields' SEP *Aristotle*, the **opening biography** and **discussion of surviving writings**, supplies fourth-century intellectual context, not an exact date for composing the elemental theory. Surviving works may be unfinished, reworked or editorially arranged; terminology and detailed interpretations require care.
- Aristotle's *On Generation and Corruption*, **Book II, Part 2**, distinguishes hot/cold and moist/dry and explains the latter through shape and boundaries; **Part 3** explicitly pairs fire hot/dry, air hot/moist, water cold/moist and earth cold/dry. This is a primary philosophical work accessed through **H. H. Joachim's English translation**, not direct inspection of an ancient Greek manuscript. Our accessible word **wet** corresponds to that translation's **moist**, not a modern humidity measurement.

Origins has no historical date range. Its exact subtitle is: "An undated prologue inspired by the four classical elements of ancient Greek natural philosophy." Its position before Stone Age does not mean Greek philosophy preceded prehistoric humanity. Century labels in the four entries date philosophical contexts, never the origin of individual physical elements.

| Aspect | Classification |
| --- | --- |
| Four-element theory | Documented Greek natural philosophy, superseded as chemistry |
| Empedocles' four roots | Fifth-century BCE Greek philosophical theory |
| Aristotle's elemental qualities | Fourth-century BCE Greek natural philosophy |

| Element-combination recipes | Symbolic or simplified game mechanics, not reconstructions of either theory |
| Modern scientific status | Fire accompanies combustion, a chemical reaction; pure water is the compound H2O; earth denotes terrestrial matter, not one chemical element; atmospheric air is a mixture of gases |

This is a particular Greek intellectual tradition, **not a universal belief of every ancient civilization or every Greek thinker**. For comparison, Chinese *wuxing* discusses five phases named wood, fire, earth, metal and water through cycles of generation and overcoming. It belongs to a distinct cultural and intellectual context; similar labels do not establish identical theories, a common origin or independent inventions of the Greek roots.

The four starters retain their IDs, categories, icons, age, starting status and recipes. Each description keeps its opening flavor sentence separate from a labeled **Historical context** summary. The Atlas supplies the verified explanation, century-level philosophical dates, textual evidence, uncertainty and citations; Origins itself remains undated. The separate Classical Antiquity chapter is a regional gameplay chapter, not a historical age that follows everywhere after the Iron Age.

The earlier blanket subtitle, Atlas placement label and chronology-table phrase describing Origins' "essences" as fictional have been clarified. References below and the [research registry](../src/game/content/research.json) support these corrections. Fictional life-making, symbolic Metal creation and unrelated gameplay gates remain disclosed as such.

## Optional Age V branches

The expansion uses the existing chapter gate and completion targets, not a new universal chronology. Knowledge, observation, maritime exchange, libraries, geometry, and arches all have earlier precedents; their Age V placement organizes regional examples rather than inventing them in Classical Antiquity.

- **Civic life:** Agora and Forum are analogical gameplay branches, not interchangeable Greek and Roman institutions. Senate and Republic refer to Roman examples with changing powers and unequal civic rights; the recipe chain does not guarantee democracy or equal participation. Rhetoric concerns persuasion, not necessarily truth.
- **Architecture:** Arch, Stoa, and Amphitheater are different building forms. Existing Aqueduct remains an Iron Age discovery and provides an optional architectural clue, not a requirement that aqueducts use arches. Stoa does not automatically mean Stoicism, and an amphitheater is not a semicircular theater.
- **Commerce and scholarship:** Coinage distinguishes a monetary system from an individual Coin; Mint omits actual production requirements. Maritime Trade uses existing Amphora and Voyage without dating sea exchange to this chapter. Library and Geometry retain earlier-precedent cautions; existing Scroll is neither duplicated nor moved out of Bronze Age.
- **Natural history:** Observation + Knowledge evokes Natural Inquiry, with an alternate Natural Philosophy route and optional Bird Inquiry. The original Fossil Inquiry remains independent of that branch. Ancient questions about birds and fossils are not modern evolutionary classification or proof that fossils inspired mythology.

Every Age V discovery now has a sourced Atlas record, with supplemental histories for existing Aqueduct, Scroll, and Coin. The [research registry](../src/game/content/research.json) records checked passages, credited authors, original URLs and access limits. All 17 expansion formula cautions have relevant background references; sources establish historical context, never the exact two-input recipes.

The expanded evidence distinguishes:

- **Greek and Roman civic contexts:** ASCSA describes the agora's political, legal, commercial and intellectual uses. The Roman Forum, Senate and Republic records distinguish excavated phases, retrospective foundation stories, changing institutions and unequal participation.
- **Earlier engineering:** Etruscan arched gates precede Roman adaptations. Aegean and Mesopotamian conduits, Assyrian works and Greek tunnels predate Roman aqueduct monuments. UNESCO's Persian qanats draw groundwater through underground galleries and shafts; they are not arched Roman bridges, and the accessed description does not establish a precise invention date.
- **Diverse inquiry and mathematics:** Stanford's Indian and Chinese philosophy articles warn against flattening different categories and periods. MacTutor's Sulbasutras, Nine Chapters and Euclid accounts describe distinct mathematical traditions; later commentary is not the first invention of its mathematics. No worldwide simultaneous Classical transition is claimed.
- **Scholarship and natural history:** Nineveh's tablet collection and earlier libraries predate Alexandria; Alexandria's founding and fate remain uncertain. Aristotle's bird descriptions include reported claims, not modern confirmation of every belief or ancient knowledge of avian dinosaur ancestry. Fossil Inquiry retains its distinction between ancient explanations and modern paleontology, including the disputed griffin hypothesis.

Agora remains a core fifth-age civic landmark. A separate optional track reuses Agora as a civic landmark, Library as a knowledge landmark, Aqueduct as an engineering landmark and Mint as an optional economic landmark. They are additive collection objectives, not new core completion requirements. Arch supports the Aqueduct objective; Stoa remains in the earlier civic collection. Aqueduct retains its Iron Age gameplay assignment, not a universal first-invention date. Supporting discoveries are gameplay membership rules, not causal claims that all conduits needed arches, libraries needed papyrus or commerce needed coins. The four additional named labs use supplied puzzle foundations and existing recipes; cited historical context stays separate from puzzle flavor. The [objective tables](gameplay.md#optional-age-v-objectives) document membership and routes; reading historical notes is separate from both challenge instructions and rewards.

## Ancient intellectual practices are not modern science

Observation, Knowledge, Natural Inquiry, Natural Philosophy and Geometry retain their existing identities and earlier-foundation cautions. Rhetoric and Logic address persuasion and inference without new generic Reason, Argument or Philosophy discoveries. Existing Fossil Inquiry is the ancient fossil-interpretation branch, not modern paleontology; the disputed Protoceratops-griffin hypothesis remains unproven.

- **Mathematical Astronomy:** Late Babylonian computed tables, Ptolemy's Alexandrian geometrical models and Aryabhata's Indian verse text show different techniques. The 499 CE text date does not begin Indian astronomy. Predictive success does not prove a model's physical cosmology; geometry is not every tradition's prerequisite.
- **Clinical Observation:** Egyptian trauma-case evidence in the Edwin Smith Papyrus predates Age V. Structured descriptions and prognoses are not modern clinical trials or proof that a treatment worked. The entry provides no medical advice and does not divide ancient cultures into a simplistic rational-versus-magical ladder.
- **Logic:** Aristotelian deduction and Chinese Mohist standards, distinctions and analogical argument are not identical systems. Valid inference does not guarantee true premises; persuasion does not guarantee valid inference. The comparison neither invents the first human reasoning nor proves cultural isolation.
- **Natural Classification:** Aristotle's animal comparisons are not Linnaean ranks, biological species definitions or evolutionary trees. Translated genus/species terms need caution. Birds' theropod ancestry is modern scientific context, not knowledge assigned to ancient observers.

These are selected Greek, Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Indian and Chinese examples, not simultaneous Classical ages or exhaustive regional histories. All four new discoveries are optional, with source-specific dates and passage-access limits. See the [source audit](research.md#intellectual-practices-audit).

## Natural materials: formation is not invention

Phase 9G adds geological histories for Clay, Rock, Sand, Limestone, Copper Ore and Iron Ore while retaining their recipes and ages. USGS clay mineralogy and BCcampus geology explain natural formation; existing archaeological and museum sources support particular human-use contexts. Flint and obsidian appear as examples within Rock, not new catalog elements. Earth remains the historical philosophical starter, distinct from Rock.

- Clay can mean fine particles or particular minerals; wetting arbitrary earth does not instantly form clay minerals or fire pottery.
- Sand is a size class with varied compositions. The dust-and-sea clue evokes sediment transport and sorting, not an automatic chemical transformation.
- Limestone commonly includes marine carbonate remains, but spring, cave and other carbonate deposits prevent a universal marine-origin claim. Natural carbonate rock differs from manufactured quicklime.
- Ore deposits predate their discovery. Bronze/Iron Age resource grants symbolize access, not geological formation or a worldwide first mine. Ore, extracted metal and alloy are distinct; reduction is not simply melting.
- Natural formation dates, archaeological use dates and widespread adoption are separate questions. Modern scientific explanations do not demonstrate that ancient craftspeople held those theories.

The sand/glass comparison does not identify a particular ancient feedstock; abstract-only pottery and plaster evidence remains explicitly limited. The [coverage audit](research.md#phase-9g-natural-history-audit) lists unresearched natural groups rather than implying that this geological batch covers all organisms and phenomena.
